# Draft a Business Review Form

Turn existing materials into proposed working methods that a business owner can review and change. The agent reads, organizes, prefills, and checks the material. The reviewer decides whether the proposed methods fit actual operations and supplies differences or unresolved decisions.

## Establish the deliverable

- Use the current request and previously confirmed corrections to establish the audience, review purpose, source boundaries, output format, and destination.
- When the user wants to review how work should be done, deliver prefilled working methods. Do not substitute a questionnaire about what is needed before implementation.
- For formatting-only edits, preserve the business content. For prompt-only requests, return a prompt. Start from the user's existing structure; change it only where mixed decisions, duplication, or readability justify doing so.
- Continue when the materials or conversation provide the necessary information. Ask only when missing input would change a business decision; complete independent work in the meantime.

## Read before prefilling

Maintain an internal comparison of confirmed requirements, current rules, historical practices, unresolved matters, and affected scenarios. Use it to ensure accuracy rather than creating another form for the user to complete.

- Apply the currently adopted rules. Use historical inputs to recover real actions, records, branches, tools, and constraints. When a current decision supersedes an older practice, retain the reason for that choice; neither recency nor document length alone establishes authority.
- Read the specified source materials and relevant attachments. A directory listing, previous summary, or generated table does not establish that the sources were reviewed. If a source is inaccessible, state the actual reading scope and its effect.
- Prefill established responsibilities, completion conditions, and division of work. Label proposed additions as suggestions. Leave genuinely unresolved people, tools, deadlines, or record locations for confirmation rather than asking the user to redefine known rules.
- Name source-supported capabilities and their business purpose accurately. Distinguish historical existence, current availability, and integration into the proposed workflow. Without evidence, describe the required business action instead of inventing a tool, interface, acceptance, or execution result.

## Organize around recognizable situations

Extract normal handling, conditional branches, waiting, handoffs, failure recovery, and completion from the sources. Include each where relevant rather than making every record and step mandatory on every occasion.

Use complete business names and observable actions, such as "which work the colleague explicitly accepted" or "whether the record was actually saved." Put technical identifiers and implementation details in supporting material only when needed. Do not make technical knowledge a prerequisite for business review.

Use one business decision per row. Related conditions of the same decision may stay together. Split independently reviewable decisions; merge repeated decisions and retain only meaningful differences elsewhere. Do not add rows, columns, or separate skills just to increase coverage counts.

Even within the same case, "how to verify the problem" and "what proves it can be closed" may require separate review. If a reviewer could accept one and reject the other, split them. If the user explicitly limits the number of rows, distinguish the decisions within the allowed space instead of collapsing them into a vague approval.

## Make each review row internally consistent

Keep the user's field names. If none are specified, use these six fields:

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Situation | A concrete situation the person doing the work can recognize. |
| Proposed way of working | A source-grounded proposal: what to inspect first, how to decide, what to do, and what to produce or do next. |
| Decision to review | The specific business choice: information scope, branch condition, responsibility, handling method, or completion evidence. |
| What to check | The named record or information source plus what to verify in it, supporting that decision rather than repeating the review question. |
| Example change request | One short example that directly changes the same decision or method, clearly labeled as an example. |
| My comments or changes | Leave blank for the reviewer's actual response. Blank does not mean approval. |

Distinguish three information treatments: reuse what is already known; ask for missing information only when needed for the current decision; consult branch-specific records only when that branch applies. Avoid copying the same complete history checklist into every row.

Examples demonstrate how to request a change; they are not user facts or completed reviewer responses. Actual responses may include agreement, a replacement instruction, an unresolved question, or a source link. Read the [review row example](references/review-row-example.md) on first use or when checking the relationship between fields.

## Check meaning before presentation

Review every row before formatting:

1. Can the named information in "What to check" support the stated decision? If not, specify the missing source and check, or rewrite or split the decision.
2. Could the example directly change this row's method, condition, or output? Replace generic requests such as "please improve this."
3. Compare against the original materials for omitted branches, exceptions, remaining responsibilities, and known capabilities. Matching section headings does not establish business coverage.
4. Can a nontechnical reviewer decide without memorizing codes? Can they review differences instead of restating established rules?
5. Are established rules, suggestions, and unresolved points distinguishable? Check for historical practices, simulated results, delivered notifications, or available tools being mistaken for acceptance or completion.

When creating spreadsheets or documents, use the relevant artifact capability available in the environment. Otherwise use suitable available tooling and disclose material verification limits. Keep widths, text wrapping, and comment space usable. Separate example text from actual response cells. Inspect the saved output and affected pages rather than treating a successful save as proof of a usable form.

Save to the requested destination and preserve originals when required. Deliver the file with a short review order and any actual source limitations. Producing a review draft does not mean the business has accepted it, tools are connected, or a real trial succeeded. Installation, publication, pushes, and external sharing remain within the user's authorized scope.
